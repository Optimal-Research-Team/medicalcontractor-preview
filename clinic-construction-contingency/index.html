Clinic Construction Contingency: Budget It Right

Clinic construction contingency protects your opening budget from hidden conditions, permit changes, and healthcare-specific construction surprises on site.

Sep 29, 2026 · 6 min read

On this page
  1. What a Clinic Construction Contingency Covers
  2. Set the Percentage Based on Project Risk
  3. Reduce Risk Before You Build
  4. Build a Contingency Process, Not Just a Line Item
  5. Know When to Protect the Schedule Over the Lowest Cost
  6. A Contingency Should Create Control

A clinic construction contingency is not an optional cushion for a project that was poorly planned. It is a controlled reserve for costs that cannot be fully confirmed until design, permitting, demolition, inspections, and construction are underway. For a physician, dentist, pharmacist, or clinic owner, the goal is not simply to finish the space. It is to open on schedule with enough working capital left to staff the practice, market services, purchase supplies, and manage the first months of operations.

A contingency that is too small can force rushed decisions, reduce finish quality, delay opening, or require additional financing. A contingency that is too large can make a promising project look unaffordable to a lender. The right number depends on the building, the scope, the lease terms, the clinical services planned, and how much has been verified before construction starts.

What a Clinic Construction Contingency Covers

In commercial healthcare buildouts, a contingency covers legitimate unknowns, not wish-list upgrades or late changes in direction. It protects the project budget when existing conditions differ from available drawings, when code officials require modifications, or when concealed conditions appear after walls, ceilings, or flooring are opened.

A typical medical office, dental clinic, urgent care center, or pharmacy may encounter aging electrical infrastructure, insufficient plumbing capacity, slab penetrations, unmarked utilities, damaged framing, fire protection changes, or HVAC equipment that cannot support the final room layout. In older retail and office buildings, these issues are common. They are also difficult to price with certainty until the contractor has access to the space and, in some cases, until selective demolition begins.

Healthcare projects add another layer of exposure. Handwashing requirements, accessibility clearances, infection-control finishes, emergency power needs, imaging shielding, medical gas, ventilation, cleanable surfaces, and occupancy classifications can affect cost after a general commercial plan would appear complete. A clinic is not simply an office with exam rooms. Its construction budget must reflect clinical operations and regulatory requirements from the beginning.

Set the Percentage Based on Project Risk

Many owners ask for a standard percentage. There is no single percentage that fits every clinic, but a practical starting range is often 5% to 10% of hard construction costs. A newer shell space with complete landlord documentation, a coordinated design, and straightforward medical office use may justify the lower end. An older building, complex renovation, tenant improvement with limited access, or specialty clinical use may require a higher reserve.

For example, a 4,000-square-foot family medicine clinic in a recently built medical office building may have fewer unknowns than a dental practice taking over a former restaurant. The dental conversion could involve extensive plumbing changes, electrical upgrades, equipment coordination, compressed air, vacuum systems, and potential slab work. The projects may have similar square footage, but they do not carry the same construction risk.

A 10% contingency is not automatically conservative if the project team has not completed due diligence. Conversely, a 5% contingency can be appropriate when the team has performed site investigations, confirmed utility capacity, coordinated equipment requirements, and resolved major code questions before pricing. The percentage should follow the evidence, not a rule of thumb copied from another project.

Separate Contingency From Owner Changes

This distinction protects both the owner and the contractor. Construction contingency addresses unforeseen conditions and defined project risk. Owner changes are decisions made after the budget or contract is established, such as adding treatment rooms, upgrading millwork, changing flooring, selecting premium lighting, or relocating equipment.

When these categories are blended together, owners lose visibility. A project can appear to be exceeding contingency when the real cause is a series of business decisions made after design approval. Keep a written change-management process that identifies whether each cost is an unforeseen condition, a code requirement, a scope gap, or an owner-requested change.

Reduce Risk Before You Build

The best way to manage contingency is not to argue over it after demolition. It is to reduce uncertainty before the construction contract is signed. That requires coordination between real estate, design, clinical operations, permitting, and construction.

Start with a site assessment that goes beyond square footage and asking rent. Review electrical service, HVAC capacity, plumbing locations, fire protection, ceiling heights, access routes, structural constraints, parking, signage, and the condition of base-building systems. If the practice will include X-ray, CT, surgical procedures, infusion, lab work, pharmacy operations, or other specialized services, those requirements need to be identified before the lease is finalized and before drawings are priced.

The lease itself matters. Owners should clarify who pays for building-system upgrades, utility connections, roof penetrations, fire alarm modifications, after-hours HVAC, permit fees, and restoration obligations. A generous tenant improvement allowance can lose value quickly if the tenant is responsible for major infrastructure that was not identified during negotiations.

Design coordination is equally important. Clinical workflow affects construction cost. A poorly planned layout may create unnecessary corridors, duplicate plumbing runs, inefficient staff movement, or oversized reception areas that reduce revenue-producing clinical space. A plan that supports patient flow, staff operations, privacy, and equipment placement can reduce avoidable construction expense while improving practice performance after opening.

Build a Contingency Process, Not Just a Line Item

A contingency reserve should be visible in the project budget and managed through a consistent approval process. It should not become an informal fund that gets spent without documentation. The owner needs to know the original contingency amount, each approved use, the remaining balance, and any risk that could still affect the budget.

A strong process includes a detailed construction estimate, a schedule of allowances, a log for requests for information and change orders, and regular cost-to-complete reporting. If an issue appears likely to consume contingency, address it early. Waiting until the end of the project reduces options and can create opening delays.

For funded projects, this discipline also supports lender confidence. Lenders want to see that the full capital requirement has been considered, including construction, professional fees, equipment, furniture, permits, technology, signage, startup costs, and operating reserves. Construction contingency is one part of the total project budget, not a substitute for adequate working capital.

Know When to Protect the Schedule Over the Lowest Cost

Every unexpected issue creates a decision: spend more now, redesign, defer work, or accept a delay. The lowest immediate cost is not always the best financial choice. If a delayed HVAC component pushes opening back by six weeks, the lost revenue, payroll commitments, lease payments, and patient disruption may cost more than an expedited replacement option.

That does not mean every premium is justified. It means decisions should be evaluated against the practice’s opening plan and economics. A specialty clinic with referral commitments, leased equipment, and hired staff may need to protect its opening date more aggressively than a small expansion that can phase work while remaining operational.

Medical Contractor approaches contingency planning as part of clinic feasibility, not as an afterthought once construction begins. The right reserve is supported by site review, clinical design, code-aware pricing, equipment coordination, and a project budget that reflects how the practice will actually operate.

A Contingency Should Create Control

A clinic buildout will always contain some uncertainty. The objective is not to pretend otherwise or to inflate every cost until the project no longer works. The objective is to identify known risks, price what can be priced, reserve funds for what cannot yet be confirmed, and make decisions with clear financial visibility.

Before signing a lease or construction contract, ask whether the contingency reflects the actual condition of the space and the complexity of the clinical program. A well-planned reserve gives your practice room to handle the unexpected without sacrificing compliance, quality, patient experience, or the capital needed to open strong.
