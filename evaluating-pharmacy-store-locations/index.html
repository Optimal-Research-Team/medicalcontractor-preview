Evaluating Pharmacy Store Locations for Profit

Evaluating pharmacy store locations requires traffic counts. Assess demand, access, reimbursement, competition, costs, and growth before each new lease.

Oct 21, 2026 · 7 min read

On this page

  2. Measure Demand Beyond Population Counts
  3. Evaluate Competition With More Precision
  4. Assess Referral Sources Without Building the Plan Around Them
  5. Test Access, Visibility, and the Pickup Experience
  6. Put Occupancy Cost in the Context of Margin
  7. Confirm Zoning, Permits, and Building Feasibility Early
  8. Score Each Site Before You Commit

A pharmacy can look busy from the street and still fail to generate enough prescription volume, front-end sales, or margin to justify its occupancy cost. Evaluating pharmacy store locations is not a real estate exercise alone. It is a business-planning decision that affects patient acquisition, payer mix, staffing, workflow, regulatory approvals, and the long-term value of the operation.

For an independent pharmacy, a specialty operation, or a retail pharmacy attached to a medical clinic, the wrong site can create a costly problem that no amount of signage or marketing will fully solve. The right site gives the business practical access to the patients, prescribers, services, and daily routines that support sustainable revenue.

Start With the Pharmacy Model, Not the Available Space

A neighborhood pharmacy, a high-volume dispensing operation, a compounding pharmacy, and a specialty pharmacy should not use the same location criteria. Before comparing properties, define the services the pharmacy will deliver and how those services will make money.

A traditional community pharmacy may depend on prescription transfer potential, walk-in convenience, immunizations, medication synchronization, and front-store purchases. A specialty pharmacy may need proximity to referral sources, patients with chronic conditions, reliable delivery routes, and sufficient private consultation space. A pharmacy integrated with a family practice, urgent care center, or medical building may benefit from a built-in referral ecosystem, but only if the arrangement complies with applicable laws and supports a genuine patient choice model.

This decision also determines the physical requirements. A site that works for standard dispensing may not have the secure receiving area, cleanable finishes, storage capacity, consultation rooms, refrigeration infrastructure, backup power planning, or controlled-substance security needed for a more complex operation. Trying to force the wrong retail shell into the business model often leads to expensive renovations and compromised workflow.

Measure Demand Beyond Population Counts

Demographics matter, but population totals alone are a weak predictor of pharmacy performance. A trade area with 20,000 residents may be more valuable than one with 60,000 if it has the right age profile, insurance coverage, healthcare utilization, and access patterns.

Look at the concentration of seniors, families, patients managing diabetes or cardiovascular conditions, and residents with limited mobility. Review household income, but do not assume that a high-income neighborhood automatically supports a stronger independent pharmacy. Insurance plan participation, copay structures, prescription benefit managers, and local healthcare access can have more influence on dispensing economics than income alone.

Daytime population is equally relevant. Employers, senior living communities, schools, transit hubs, and medical offices can create recurring prescription and immunization demand. However, a pharmacy serving commuters needs easy entry, visible parking, and a quick pickup experience. A location near a hospital may generate opportunity, but it can also place the business beside established health-system pharmacies with stronger referral relationships and purchasing power.

Define the True Trade Area

Do not draw a simple radius around the property and call it the market. Major roads, rail lines, traffic patterns, bridges, parking constraints, and neighborhood boundaries can dramatically limit how far patients are willing to travel for routine prescriptions.

For many community pharmacies, convenience is the deciding factor. Patients commonly choose the location that is on the way home, adjacent to their physician, near their grocery store, or easy to access after a medical appointment. Study drive times at the hours patients will actually visit, including morning, evening, and weekend periods. A site with strong traffic volume is less useful when drivers cannot safely enter, turn left into the center, or leave without a frustrating detour.

Evaluate Competition With More Precision

Counting nearby pharmacies is necessary, but it is not enough. A market can support several pharmacies if each serves a distinct patient base or offers a differentiated service model. Conversely, one well-established chain pharmacy with convenient parking and long operating hours can make a smaller operation difficult to launch.

Identify every competitor within the realistic trade area, including grocery-store pharmacies, big-box retailers, independent pharmacies, mail-order alternatives, clinic-owned dispensaries, and health-system locations. Then assess what they actually offer. Are they open evenings? Do they provide delivery, compounding, travel health services, durable medical equipment, or specialty medication coordination? Are patients complaining about wait times, access, or service quality?

The goal is not to avoid all competition. It is to avoid entering a market with no clear operational advantage. A new pharmacy should be able to answer a practical question: why will patients and prescribers choose this location rather than the established option two minutes away?

Assess Referral Sources Without Building the Plan Around Them

Nearby physicians, dentists, urgent care centers, fertility clinics, oncology practices, and senior care providers can strengthen a pharmacy location. They may create natural demand for vaccines, medication counseling, adherence support, specialty prescriptions, and delivery services. But referral assumptions must be tested carefully.

Ask how many providers are nearby, what specialties they represent, where their patients live, and whether they already have preferred pharmacy relationships. A large medical building is not automatically a strong pharmacy opportunity if patients leave through a different exit, parking is fragmented, or the building has a pharmacy tenant with exclusive rights.

When considering a medical plaza or clinic-adjacent site, review the lease, tenant roster, and any exclusivity provisions early. A landlord may restrict pharmacy use, limit signage, control delivery access, or grant another tenant rights that affect your business. These details should be resolved before significant design, permitting, or financing work begins.

Test Access, Visibility, and the Pickup Experience

Pharmacy customers value convenience because prescription pickup is usually one task among many. They may be ill, caring for a child, leaving a physician appointment, or trying to fit an errand into a short lunch break. The property must make the visit simple.

Evaluate parking availability, accessible spaces, curb cuts, pedestrian safety, transit access, storefront visibility, signage rights, and the ability to install a drive-thru if that is part of the model. A drive-thru can be valuable in certain markets, but it adds zoning, site-planning, construction, staffing, and queuing considerations. It should be supported by patient demand and operational capacity, not added because a competitor has one.

Inside the space, plan for a clear path from entry to prescription drop-off, waiting, consultation, front-end retail, and exit. If the pharmacy will administer vaccines or offer clinical services, private rooms must be easy to find without disrupting the dispensing area. Good patient flow reduces congestion, protects privacy, and helps the team serve more people with less friction.

Put Occupancy Cost in the Context of Margin

A low base rent is not necessarily a good deal. A cheaper site with poor access, weak visibility, inadequate electrical capacity, or a restrictive use clause can cost more than a stronger location with higher rent. Evaluate total occupancy cost, including common-area charges, taxes, utilities, signage, tenant improvements, security requirements, insurance, and likely renewal increases.

Then compare that cost with a realistic revenue and gross-margin forecast. Pharmacy economics are heavily influenced by reimbursement, purchasing terms, inventory carrying costs, shrink, labor, and service mix. The store needs enough prescription volume and ancillary revenue to support its fixed costs without relying on overly optimistic assumptions.

A lender-ready business plan should model conservative, expected, and downside scenarios. Account for ramp-up time. It may take months to build patient awareness, establish prescriber relationships, transfer prescriptions, and reach stable volume. The lease term, personal guarantee, renewal options, and tenant-improvement allowance should reflect that risk.

Confirm Zoning, Permits, and Building Feasibility Early

Many pharmacy projects stall because the team signs a lease before confirming whether the space can legally and physically support the intended operation. Zoning, local building codes, fire requirements, accessibility rules, pharmacy board requirements, health department requirements, and controlled-substance security standards must be considered together.

A former retail unit may still require substantial upgrades. Common issues include insufficient electrical service for refrigeration and equipment, inadequate HVAC, limited plumbing, noncompliant washroom access, poor loading access, and lack of secure storage. If compounding, sterile processes, or specialty handling are planned, the requirements can become more demanding.

A pre-lease feasibility review should identify likely permitting timelines, construction scope, landlord obligations, and conditions that need to be written into the lease. This is where an integrated healthcare real estate and construction team can prevent a location decision from becoming an expensive correction project.

Score Each Site Before You Commit

The strongest decision is rarely based on one standout feature. Use a weighted scorecard that reflects the pharmacy’s model and financial priorities. Compare projected demand, competitive position, referral potential, patient access, parking, rent, construction cost, zoning risk, visibility, delivery logistics, and expansion capacity.

Give greater weight to the factors that cannot be fixed after opening. You can improve merchandising, add staff, and refine marketing. You cannot easily move a poorly placed entrance, create missing parking, remove a lease restriction, or change a weak trade area.

A site should also support the next phase of the business. Consider whether there is room for additional consultation services, expanded inventory, clinical programs, private vaccination rooms, or delivery operations. Growth does not require oversized space on day one, but it does require a location that will not force a premature and disruptive relocation.

The best pharmacy site is the one that makes it easier to serve patients well while protecting the economics of the operation. Before signing a letter of intent or lease, validate the demand, site feasibility, build-out cost, and operating assumptions as one connected plan. That disciplined work gives the pharmacy a stronger opening position and gives the owner far more control over the investment that follows.
